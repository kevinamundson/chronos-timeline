§. IIII. *Of the name and meaning of the words, Law, and Right.*

## p01715 · sig. 186a

The word Lex, or Law, is not always taken alike, but is diversely, and in an indifferent sense used. For if we consider it at large, it may be understood for any rule prescribing a necessary mean, order, and method, for the attaining of an end. And So the rules of Grammar, or other Arts, are called Laws *Lawes*. Or it is taken for any private ordinance of Superiors *Superiours* to Inferiors *Inferiours*: for the commandments of Tyrants, which they cause to be observed by force, for their decrees do also usurp that title, according to the general acceptation of the word Law: of which *Isaiah* *Esay**, Woe unto* *them that decree wicked decrees, and write grievous things. *Likewise, the word is used for the tumultuary resolutions of the people. For such constitutions does Aristotle also call laws, though evil and insufficient. *Mala lex est quae tumultuary posita* *est; It is an ill law that is made tumultuously. *So as all ordinances, good or evil, are called by the name of laws.

## p01716 · sig. 186b

The word Law is also taken for the moral habit of our mind, which does( as it were) command our thoughts, words, and actions: framing and fashioning them according to it self, as to their pattern and platform. And thus the law of the flesh which the Divines *Diuines* call legem fomitis, is ("the law of the tinder [of sin] is [this];") to be understood. For every law is a kind of pattern of that which is done according unto it: in which sense as * elsewhere, this moral habit or disposition of the heart is called the frame or figmentum of the heart: So in St. Paul to the Romans *Romanes* it is called a *Law. But I see another law in my members, rebelling against the law of my mind, and leading me captive unto the law of sin. *Again, the nature and inclinations of all creatures are sometime called a laws, So far as they agree with the reason of the law eternal; as the law of a Lyon, to be fierce or valiant.

## p01717 · sig. 186b

Also private contracts among Merchants and other Tradesmen, do often put on the name of laws. But law commonly and properly is taken, for a right rule, prescribing a necessary mean, for the good of a Common-wealth, or Civil community. The rest, to wit, the commandments of Tyrants, &c. which have not the common good for their end, but being leges iniquae, are by Thomas called *violentiae magis quam leges; rather compulsions then laws: *And whatsoever is not just, S t. Augustine does not allow for laws, howsoever established: for he calls them *iniquae hominum constituta, quae nec jura dicenda, nec putanda sunt; The unjust constitutions of men which are neither to be termed nor thought laws. *For says ARISTOTLE, *Legaliaiusta sunt factius, & conseruativa foelicitatis; Just laws are the workers and preservers of happiness: *because by them we are directed *ad vitam quietam, to a quiet* life, according to Cicero. Yea, *to life ever lasting*, according to the Scriptures. For the end of the law, says Plato, is God and his worship. *Finis legis Deus & cultus eius* ("The end of the law is God and his worship.")*.* Lex, or the Law is So called by the *Latins* *Latines** a legendo*, or *a ligando, of reading *or *binding: Leges quia lectae & 〈◊ 〉 〈◊ 〉, *says Varro; For after Laws were written and published, all men might read them, and behold in them whereto they were bound. The other *Etymology, a ligando*, is no less agreeable with the nature of a Law: whence in the Scripture it is called also a yoke, and a band: as *confregerunt jugum, diruperunt vincula*: they have broken the yoke, they have broken the bands. And in the second *Psalm, Dirumpamus vincula eorum, & proijciamus a nobis funs* *ipsorum; Let us break their bands in sunder, and cast away their cords from vs.*

## p01718 · sig. 186b

The Covenant it is called, because of the conditional promises of God: and because of Gods peoples voluntary submission of them selves unto it: for which word the Septuagint, and the Epistle to the Hebrews *Hebrewes*, use the word 〈◊ 〉, *a Testament or last will*: which name it has, because it is not otherwise effectual for our salvation, but in respect of the death of the Testator, for without the death of the Testator, the Testament is of no force: as *Heb* *Hebr**.. 9. 17*. it is said, *Testamentum in mortuis ratumest* ("A testament is ratified in the dead.").

## p01719 · sig. 186b

The Hebrews call the law Torah *Thorah* of teaching, because every man is thereby taught his duty, both to God and Men. The Greeks *Greekes* call it Nomos of distributing, because it distributes to every man his own due; the power of the law is the power of God: Justice being an attribute proper unto God himself. *Imperium legis imperium Dei est; The reign of the law, is the reign of God.*

## p01720 · sig. 186b

Law in general is thus defined by the *Philosophers: Lex est vitae regula, praecipiens quae sunt 〈◊ 〉, & quae fugienda; Law is the rule of life, commanding what to follow, and what to 〈◊ 〉, *or *Lex est omnium divinarum & humanarum 〈◊ 〉 Regina; Law is the Queen *or *Princess of things both humane and divine. *But this description is grounded upon the opinion of inevitable fate. Law is the very wisdom of Nature: the reason and understanding of the prudent: and the rule of right and wrong. For as a right line is called *Index sui & curui*, the demonstration of it self, and of the crooked: So is the law, the Judge and measure of right and wrong.

## p01721 · sig. 187a

M r. Hooker calls the Law a directive rule to goodness of operation: and though law as touching the substance and essence, consist in understanding: *Concludit tamen actum voluntatis; Yet it comprehends the act of our will. *The word Ius is also diversely taken, as sometime for the matter of the law and for common right: sometime for the law it self: as Ius Civil, or *Ius gentium. Isidore* ("The law of nations. Isidore.")* *distinguishes the two general words Ius and Fas: whereof Ius, says he, has reference to men, Fas to God. *Fas lex divina, Ius lex humana. *To go over another mans field, is permitted, by Gods law, not by mans; and therefore in a thing out of controversy, Virgil used both those words: as *Fas & jura sinunt*, God and men permit.

## p01722 · sig. 187a

The word Ius or Right, is derived or taken from the old substantive Noun jussus, a bidding or commandment: or perhaps from the Greek *Greeke* 〈◊ 〉, which is the name of Jupiter, or of the Latine genitive case Jovis *Iouis*, because as the Scripture speaks, *the judgement is Gods*. For as it is certain that ius-jur andum came of Jovis-iurandum *Iouis-iurandum*, ( for So we find it written in Nonius out of the ancient, in which sense the Scripture calls it juramentum Iehovae *Iehouae*) So also we may say, that Ius came of *Jovis, quia Jovis* ("of Jove, because [it comes] from Jove") est: because as God is the Author, and Pattern, and Maintainer of right, So also in his Vicegerents the Magistrates, he is the pronouncer and executor of right. Of this Ius the just are denominated, *justus a jure*, and *justitia a justo; The right gives name to the righteous: *and *justice takes her name from the just.*
