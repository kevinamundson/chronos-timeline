§. IIII. *Of the beginning of Nobility: and of the vain vaunt thereof without virtue.*

## p01413 · sig. 141a

AND with this Supreme Rule and Kingly authority began also other degrees and differences among Subjects *Subiects*. For Princes made election of others by the same Rule, by which themselves were chosen; unto whom they gave place, trust, and power. From which employments and Offices sprung those Titles, and those degrees of Honour, which have continued from Age to Age to these days. But this Nobility, or difference from the Vulgar, was not in the beginning given to the Succession of Blood, but to Succession of Virtue, as hereafter may be proved. Though at length it was sufficient for those whose Parents were advanced, to be known for the Sons of such Fathers: and So there needed then no endeavour of well-doing at all, or any contention for them to excel, upon whom glory or worldly Nobility necessarily descended. Yet hereof had Nobility denomination in the beginning, That such as excelled others in virtue, were So called: *Hinc dictus Nobilis, quasi virtute pro alijs notabilis* ("Hence a man is called Noble, as if notable by virtue above others.")*. *But after such time as the deserved Honour of the Father was given in reward to his Posterity, Saint Jerome *Hierome* judged of the Succession in this manner: Nihil aliud *video in Nobilitate appetendum, nisi quod Nobiles quadam necessitate constringantur, ne ab antiquorum probitate degenerent; I see no other thing to be affected in Nobility, then that Noblemen are by a kind of necessity bound, not to degenerate from the virtue of their Ancestors* *Ancesters**. *For if Nobility be *Virtus & antiquae divitiae; Virtue and ancient riches, *then to exceed in all those things which are extra hominem, asriches ("outside man, as riches [are];"), power, glory, and the like, do no otherwise define Nobility, then the word( animal) alone does define a reasonable man. Or if honour( according to L. Vives *Viues*) be a witness of virtue and well-doing: and Nobility( after Plutarch) the continuance of virtue in a Race or Linage: then are those in whom Virtue is extinguished, but like unto painted and printed Papers, which ignorant men worship in stead of Christ, our Lady, and other Saints: men, in whom there remain but the dregs and vices of ancient Virtue: Flowers, and Herbs *Herbes*, which by change of soil and want of manuring are turned to Weeds. For what is found prays-worthy in those waters, which had their beginning out of pure Fountains *Fountaines*, if in all the rest of their course they run foul, filthy, and defiled? *Ex terra fertili producitur aliquando 〈◊ 〉 venenosa* ("From fertile earth there is sometimes produced [illegible] a poisonous [plant],")*,* *& ex terra sterili pretiosum aurum; Out of fruitful ground arises sometimes poisoning Henbane: and out of barren soil precious Gold. *For as all things consist of matter and form, So does Charron( in his Chapter of Nobility) call the Race and Linage but the matter of Nobility: the form( which gives life and perfect being) he makes to be Virtue, and Quality, profitable to the Commonweal. For he is truly and entirely Noble, who makes a singular profession of public Virtue, serving his Prince and Country, and being descended of Parents and Ancestors that have done the like. And although that Nobility, which the same Author calls personal, ( the same which our selves acquire by our Virtue and well deservings) cannot be balanced with that which is both natural by Descent, and also personal; yet if Virtue be wanting to the natural, then is the personal and acquired Nobility by many degrees to be preferred: For( says this Charron) this Honour( to wit) by Descent, may light upon such a one, as in his own nature is a true Villain. There is also a third Nobility which he calls Nobility in Parchment, bought with Silver or favour: and these be indeed but Honours of affection, which Kings with the change of their fancies wish they knew well how to wipe off again. But surely, if we had as much sense of our degenerating in worthiness, as we have of vanity in deriving our selves of such and such Parents, we should rather know such Nobility( without Virtue) to be shame and dishonour, then Nobleness, and glory to vaunt thereof. *What calamity is wanting*( says 〈◊ 〉) *to him that is borne in sin, of a Potsherd* *body and barren mind*? for( according to the same Father: ) *Dele fucum fugacis honoris huius, & male coronatae nitorem gloria, &c. Wipe away the painting of this fleeting honour, and the glittering of the ill-crowned glory, that then thou may consider thy self 〈◊ 〉: for 〈◊ 〉 came naked out of thy Mothers womb. Came thou thence with thy Mitre, or glistening with Jewels* *Iewels**, or garnished with Silks* *Silkes**, or adorned with Feathers, or stuffed with Gold? If thou scatter and blow away all these by thy consideration as certain morning clouds, which do or will soon passe over, thou shalt meet with a naked, and poor, and wretched, and miserable man, and blushing, because he is naked, and weeping because he is borne, and repining because he is borne to labour, and not to honour.*

## p01414 · sig. 141b

For, as touching the matter of all men, there is no difference between it and dust: which if *thou do not believe*( says Saint Chrysostom *CHRYSOSTOME*) *look into the Sepulchres* *and Monuments of thy Ancestors, and they shall easily persuade thee by their own example, that thou art dust and dirt: So that if man seem more Noble and beautiful then dust, this proceeds not from the diversity of his Nature, but from the cunning of his Creator.*

## p01415 · sig. 141b

And howsoever the custom of the World have made it good, that Honours be cast by birth upon unworthy Issues: yet Solomon *Salomon*( as wise as any King) reprehends the same in his Fellow-Princes: *There is an evil*( says he) *that I have seen under the Sun,* *as an error that proceeds from the face of him that rules. Folly is set in great excellency.*
